A policy nobody reads is a policy nobody follows

Most AI policies end up as a PDF in a shared drive. Staff hit a question mid-task — can I put this in Copilot? — and either guess, or email someone and wait two days.

This sits between those two outcomes. It answers in seconds, strictly from your documents, and hands off to a named person the moment it isn't certain.

What makes it trustworthy

It only knows your documents

No general knowledge about AI, no advice from the internet, no filling gaps with something plausible. If your policy doesn't cover it, it says so.

A verdict, not an essay

Green, Yellow or Red, then two sentences of plain English and the document it came from. Readable on a phone between meetings.

It knows when to stop

Anything ambiguous, prohibited or beyond the documents ends with a named person to contact. It removes hesitation; it doesn't replace judgement.

It keeps a record

Every question is logged. Reviewed monthly, that log is a live map of where your policy is unclear — written by the people who actually have to follow it.

Build it yourself

You don't need a developer, and for most organisations you don't need me either. Two versions below. Before you start, you need your AI policy as text you can copy, and a named person willing to be the contact for anything unclear.

If you have supporting documents — an approved tools list, a data classification guide — gather those too. They make the answers noticeably better, because most real questions turn on the detail in them rather than on the policy itself.

Option 1 — the straightforward one

An assistant your staff can ask, that hands off to a person when it isn't sure. No log, no admin side. About fifteen minutes.

  1. Open the AI tool your organisation already uses and create a new custom assistant — a Custom GPT in ChatGPT, a Project in Claude, a Gem in Gemini, or an agent in Copilot.
  2. Copy the prompt below into its instructions.
  3. Replace the highlighted placeholders with your organisation's name, your policy owner, and their email.
  4. Paste your full policy text where the prompt says to.
  5. Save it, then share the link — in Teams, on your intranet, or in an email signature.
Prompt — Option 1 
You are the AI Policy Assistant for [ORGANISATION].

Answer only from the policy below. Never use outside knowledge about AI
tools, vendors, or best practice — if it is not in the policy, you do not
know it.

Reply in this format every time:

**[GREEN / YELLOW / RED]**
Two or three sentences, plain language, explaining why.
*Source: [section or heading you relied on]*

GREEN  = clearly allowed
RED    = clearly prohibited
YELLOW = ambiguous, not addressed, or depends on specifics

If the policy does not clearly cover the question, say so and answer
YELLOW. Never fill a gap with general knowledge or a reasonable guess.

End every YELLOW or RED answer with:
"Please check with [NAME] at [EMAIL] before proceeding."

Do not ask for or repeat real names, client records, donor details, or
other personal information. If a question contains them, answer the
general question and remind the person to leave personal details out.

--- POLICY BEGINS ---
[PASTE THE FULL POLICY TEXT HERE]
--- POLICY ENDS ---

Paste the policy into the instructions rather than attaching it as a file. Attached files get searched in fragments, so the assistant sometimes misses a relevant clause. Pasted text is always fully in view. For policies under about twenty pages this makes a real difference to accuracy.

Option 2 — with a question log

Everything above, plus a record of what people are asking. This is the version worth building, because the log is what tells you where your policy is unclear.

The assistant can't email anyone or keep records itself — each conversation is private to the person having it. So the logging is done by a form you already have the tools to build.

If you use Build the form in For the email alert
Microsoft 365 Microsoft Forms — responses collect in Excel automatically A Power Automate flow: when a new response is submitted, send an email
Google Workspace Google Forms — link responses to a Sheet In the Sheet: Tools → Notification settings → notify on form submit

Four fields is plenty: what you wanted to know, what the assistant said, your name and email (optional — leaving it optional gets you more honest questions), and whether it's urgent.

Then set up the assistant exactly as in Option 1, using this prompt instead:

Prompt — Option 2 
You are the AI Policy Assistant for [ORGANISATION].

SOURCES, in order of authority:
1. AI Use Policy — this governs. If anything below conflicts with it, it wins.
2. [Approved Tools List / Data Guide / other supporting documents]

Answer only from these documents. Never use outside knowledge about AI
tools, vendors, or best practice — if it is not written here, you do not
know it. If a supporting document contradicts the policy, follow the
policy and say you noticed the conflict.

Reply in this format every time:

**[GREEN / YELLOW / RED]** · Confidence: [High / Low]
Two or three sentences, plain language.
*Source: [which document, and which section]*

GREEN  = clearly allowed
RED    = clearly prohibited
YELLOW = ambiguous, not addressed, or depends on specifics

Use Low confidence whenever you had to infer, extrapolate, or read between
the lines — not only when the documents are silent.

For any YELLOW, RED, or Low-confidence answer, add:

"This one isn't clear-cut. Please log it here so [NAME] can give you a
definitive answer: [FORM LINK]"

Then write a two-sentence summary of the question they can paste straight
into the form.

Do not ask for or repeat real names, client records, donor details, or
other personal information. If a question contains them, answer the
general question and remind the person to leave personal details out.

--- DOCUMENTS BEGIN ---
[PASTE POLICY AND SUPPORTING DOCUMENTS HERE, EACH CLEARLY LABELLED]
--- DOCUMENTS END ---

Using the log — the part most people skip

The answers help one person for one minute. The log is what improves the policy, and it's worth putting a recurring half hour in the diary for it.

  1. Read the month's questions in one sitting. Themes are obvious in a batch and invisible one at a time.
  2. Look for repeats. The same question asked three times is a gap in the policy, not three confused people.
  3. Edit the document, not the assistant. Add the missing clause to your policy and the assistant improves immediately — and stays auditable, because every answer still traces back to an approved document.
  4. Tell people what changed. Staff who see their question turn into policy ask more questions. That's the flywheel.

Don't let the assistant learn from its own answers. It's tempting, but a policy tool has to be auditable — you need to be able to say why it gave an answer and point at the clause. Keep the humans in the loop and the documents as the single source of truth.

What the DIY version can't do

  • The log is opt-in. You'll see what people bother to submit — expect around half. Still enough to spot themes.
  • Everyone needs a seat. A shared assistant requires paid accounts. Staff without one can paste the whole prompt into any free AI tool instead.
  • Format isn't guaranteed. A prompt asks for consistency; purpose-built software enforces it. Expect the occasional answer that drifts.
  • No central admin view. Your spreadsheet is the dashboard.

For most organisations that's a fair trade, and either version beats the policy nobody opens. When those limits start to bite — usually when you want to see everything being asked rather than only what gets reported — that's the point where a purpose-built version earns its keep.
